About these detections
Satellites and aircraft capture a snapshot, not a continuous record. A plume shows emissions at one moment in time, and no detection does not mean a landfill isn’t emitting methane.

Detection limits: Instruments can only see plumes above a certain size. Smaller releases, which can add up, often go undetected, and the smallest detectable plume varies by instrument.
Coverage: Each landfill is observed only occasionally, and some are not observed at all.
Observation conditions: Clouds, wind, terrain and surface brightness affect whether a plume can be seen and how precisely it can be measured.
Estimates: Emission rates are estimates; the ± value shows their uncertainty.

Questions about the data or how to interpret these detections? Contact Carbon Mapper at info@carbonmapper.org.
What multiple plumes tell us
Landfills rarely leak from just one spot. Methane builds up throughout the buried waste and escapes wherever it finds a path. Common sources include:
• Cracks and gaps in cover soil, especially on steep side slopes
• The active working face, where fresh waste is exposed each day
• Newer areas without gas collection. Federal rules give operators up to five years after waste is placed before collection is required in active areas.
• Damaged or poorly tuned gas wells and wellheads
• Leaking pipes and leachate system parts like sumps and cleanouts
• Flares or engines that are offline, letting gas escape that should be destroyed
How better rules could fix this
These leaks are preventable. Stronger landfill rules could:
• Require gas collection to be installed sooner in new waste areas
• Require more frequent, more sensitive monitoring for leaks
• Require operators to investigate and repair when satellites or aircraft detect large plumes. EPA’s Super Emitter Program covers oil and gas, but not landfills.
• Set stronger standards for cover materials

Why does methane matter?
Methane is an extremely potent greenhouse gas — about 80 times more powerful than carbon dioxide in the short term. Large methane releases from landfills can also signal the presence of other harmful pollutants, including benzene, hydrogen sulfide, and PFAS. The good news is that proven, cost-effective solutions exist to dramatically reduce these emissions.
